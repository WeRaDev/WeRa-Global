# Transcribe, understand and clear the transcription

The recording lays out a product and service model for **deliverable-centred AI automation**. The immediate project is a chatbot-led discovery process that collects operational data, estimates automation ROI, and becomes the entry point to deploying an agent-enabled automation system for a customer.[^1_1]

## Cleaned transcription

### Working note: Automation Center / Intelligent Project

We are working on a chatbot based on an interview methodology or playbook. Its purpose is to ask a small number of questions, clarify the company’s current operations, and estimate investment requirements and expected returns from automation.

The chatbot should probably use around ten questions, although we may be able to reduce that number. The answers should give us enough information to form reliable ROI expectations.

The new task is to create a spreadsheet or database that contains information about the employer, employees, roles, workflows, and deliverables. Its main purpose is to calculate ROI in terms of time and money.

If we save time, we need a clear way to convert that saved time into monetary value. If we save money directly, that is already measurable. The spreadsheet should include both numerical values and contextual information.

The chatbot will be connected to the spreadsheet. Its main function is to ask questions and fill in the answers. The chatbot provides structure for the conversation and captures context. Then an agent analyses the answers and asks additional questions where clarification is needed.

We want clarity not only about the company’s situation, but also about the certainty of the customer’s answers and estimates. We can send a link to the chatbot to prospective customers, gather data, run calculations or scripts, and return an initial estimate.

This should differentiate us from companies that provide vague or unreliable ROI estimates. Our positioning is efficient automation based on evidence and measurable outcomes.

The first round of interviews happens through the chatbot. The second round happens once automation begins, when we start handling and labelling real data points. Automating data collection itself becomes the first practical entry point into the wider automation programme.

The customer receives a link to the chatbot and then access to the automation system. Agents will function similarly to Odoo users: they will have user identities and roles inside the system. Customers will also connect through Odoo or a comparable business workspace.

The central question is: **what exactly are we automating?**

The answer is: we automate deliverables.

For every role, we identify the deliverables a person produces. A deliverable may be fully automated, partially automated, validated by a person, or reviewed by a person. It can be a document, report, piece of code, sale, record, request, decision, or any other registered and verifiable result that is delivered internally or externally.

We build agents for the portions of each deliverable that can and should be automated. The objective is not necessarily to eliminate the human role. Instead, automation should reduce the human time required—potentially by around 60% to 80%—while keeping the person responsible for verification, labelling, approval, and final accountability.

Humans retain authority over the output. They verify and label the parts of the deliverable, effectively signing off on them.

From deliverables, we derive workflows. The workflow should not simply copy the company’s existing process. It should become a more efficient workflow that supports delivery. This is how we justify the ROI: we do not automate inefficient routines exactly as they are; we redesign them around the outcome that must be delivered.

Delivery is the operational core of the company. It includes the internal and external supply chain and the sequence of activities that produces a result. A result may be code, a sent report, a completed sale, a customer response, or any accountable output.

A deliverable is the concrete artifact or result of that work.

Some existing workflows are merely rituals: “do research,” “prepare a document,” or “perform activities and report them.” We should focus on efficiency by starting from the required deliverable and creating workflows that support it.

We measure the deliverable’s place in the value chain by looking at the human participation required across the company. We do not examine only one role; we capture all relevant roles and their deliverables. Then we identify what can be automated. Where there are outdated or archaic deliverables, we automate or redesign them and extract value in the form of saved time, freed capacity, or released resources.

Technically, we deploy a dedicated server or standalone machine containing an agent framework with business-software capabilities. It can connect to different platforms and legacy systems. It includes an internal workspace—potentially cloud-based—for collaboration among people and agents.

The system contains human users and agent users. Agents are involved in company activities according to their assigned tasks. In principle, anything a person can do within the system, an agent may also be able to do, subject to permissions and verification.

Humans teach agents by working through the software and demonstrating the process. The system uses those interactions to help agents perform procedures.

For example, in an email system, a user performs normal email tasks while an agent supports the process. The agent can act as an email assistant, prepare messages, manage communication chains, and provide reports for the human to verify. The human remains the person who confirms that the result is legitimate.

The chatbot questionnaire is the gateway or funnel into the service. We collect information, identify automatable deliverables, estimate potential savings or additional earnings, calculate the expected ROI, and show the cost of implementation.

Once the customer subscribes, we deploy their automation server or environment and charge an automation fee plus a maintenance fee.

Pricing should preferably be based on deliverables rather than simply on the number of workflows, because deliverables are closer to measurable outcomes and customer value. Pricing could potentially include a fixed implementation fee, an ongoing maintenance fee, and possibly a value-linked or delivery-linked component. We still need to define the cost model properly.

## What the note means

The concept has four connected layers:


| Layer | Purpose | Output |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Discovery chatbot | Ask a short, structured set of business questions | Initial operational data and confidence level |
| ROI database | Convert role, workflow, cost, time, and deliverable data into calculations | Automation opportunity and ROI estimate |
| Deliverable mapping | Identify what each role must produce and how it is validated | Automation candidates and redesigned workflows |
| Agent workspace | Run the approved automations with human oversight | Measurable, auditable delivery support |

The strongest idea in the recording is this:

> Do not sell generic “AI automation.” Sell the measurable improvement of specific business deliverables.

That provides a more credible commercial logic than “we automate tasks.” A task can be vague and endless; a deliverable has an owner, a recipient, a quality standard, a completion condition, a cost, and an observable business outcome.

## Sharpened positioning

A clearer version of the proposition could be:

> We identify the business deliverables that consume the most operational effort, redesign the workflows around those outcomes, and deploy supervised AI agents that reduce manual work while preserving human validation and accountability.

Or, for a landing page:

> **Measure before you automate.**\
> Our assessment chatbot maps your roles, deliverables, and operational costs. It identifies where AI agents can reduce manual effort, estimates the expected return, and creates a practical automation roadmap—without blindly reproducing inefficient processes.

## Decisions to make next

1. **Define the unit of analysis.**\
Decide precisely whether the core unit is a deliverable, a deliverable type, a workflow, a role, or a business process. The recording strongly supports “deliverable” as the primary unit.
2. **Create the assessment data model.**\
At minimum, capture company, department, role, deliverable, recipient, frequency, time spent, labour cost, systems used, input sources, validation requirements, current error/rework rate, automation suitability, implementation effort, and confidence score.
3. **Separate estimate from commitment.**\
The chatbot should produce an indicative opportunity estimate, not a guaranteed financial outcome. The second discovery stage should validate the assumptions against real work samples, logs, and actual data.
4. **Define the human-control model.**\
Specify when an agent may draft, execute, escalate, request approval, or act independently. The recording’s core principle is human verification and accountability.
5. **Design the commercial model.**\
A sensible structure appears to be: paid assessment or qualification stage; implementation fee per automation package or deliverable family; recurring platform, hosting, monitoring, and maintenance fee; optional performance-based component only where savings can be objectively measured.
6. **Make the distinction explicit.**\
Your differentiator is not simply the chatbot. It is the chain: structured discovery → confidence-weighted ROI model → deliverable-based redesign → supervised deployment → measurable operational results.

## Suggested first chatbot questions

A short initial version could ask:

1. What business outcome or deliverable does this role produce?
2. Who receives or depends on that deliverable?
3. How often is it produced?
4. How much time does it take on average?
5. How many people contribute to it?
6. Which systems, documents, inboxes, or data sources are involved?
7. Which steps are repetitive, rule-based, or template-driven?
8. Which steps require human judgement, approval, or legal responsibility?
9. What happens when the deliverable is late, incomplete, or incorrect?
10. What would improve if this deliverable required 50% less manual effort?

The result should be an **automation opportunity profile**, rather than only a single ROI number: potential value, confidence level, recommended automation scope, human-approval points, estimated implementation complexity, and next validation steps.
